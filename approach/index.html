Approach

Finished when the team can run it.

One operator on every engagement. Built from the food, the room, and the staffing you actually have — not from last year’s trends. The same doctrine covers beverage programs and Neat AI.

Most consultancies sell a deck. This practice sells an operation: specs, costings, training, and — where it applies — the guest and staff systems that keep the room answering when the floor is full. The sequence is short on purpose.

1 · A call, not a discovery invoice

Fifteen minutes. The concept, the timeline, what is not working. If it is not a fit, you leave knowing that. If it is, you get a written proposal — scope, timeline, fee — before anyone is on the clock.

2 · Fixed scope before anything starts

Opening programs run 12–16 weeks. Refreshes 6–10. Prep-lab 8–14. RTD 14–24. Advocacy per campaign. Advisory monthly. Neat AI is scoped the same way: named modules, named deliverables, no open-ended “phase zero.”

3 · Built from the room

Menus come from the kitchen, the architecture, and the guest — not from a borrowed list. Guest systems come from the questions you already get and the voice you already have. If it does not survive a Friday, it does not ship.

4 · Costed, specified, written down

Every drink has a pour-cost target. Every SOP has an owner. Every AI answer has a source. Prep is treated as infrastructure, not spectacle — a rotovap that does not pay for itself stays in the catalog; a chatbot that invents hours does not go live.

5 · Training in the building

In-person where the drinks are. On the tools where the messages are. Train-the-trainer so the next hire is not a consultancy event. The team gets called by name in the audit a year later.

6 · Handoff the house owns

Recipes, costing sheets, prep workflows, training documents, prompts, and knowledge bases — yours. The bar should not depend on the consultant after the consultant leaves. Neither should the inbox.
