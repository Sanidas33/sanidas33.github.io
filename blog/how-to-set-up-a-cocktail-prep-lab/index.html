How to set up a cocktail prep lab.

By Andreas Sanidiotis · November 27, 2026 · 9 min read

A cocktail prep lab is not a rotary evaporator with a personality. It is the place — a corner, a lowboy, or a room — where the work that should not happen during service actually happens: syrups, acids, batches, clarifications, and anything else the menu quietly depends on. Most bars already have a version of this. It is unlabeled, it lives above the glassware, and nobody can say when it was made. This is how to set one up so service can trust it.

The note is written from the floor. The public record behind the practice is a World’s 50 Best #25 bar team. Nothing here is a shopping list for its own sake. If a tool does not change a drink you already sell, or give a shift back its minutes, it stays in the catalog. When the corner is no longer enough — several outlets, a hotel drawing from one commissary — the designed room is a different engagement: prep-lab design and build.

Start with the menu, not the equipment

Write down what the current list, or the list you are about to print, actually requires before the doors open. Group it. Fresh juice and citrus stock. Syrups and cordials. A dairy or a clarified product, if you have one. A batched sour or a highball. Garnishes that die if they sit out. Anything acid-adjusted.

Then write the volume. Not a yearly dream. A Saturday. How many of each drink leave the well, and how much prep that is in liters, not in vibes. A lab without a par is a hobby. The labor side of the same idea is in your labor problem is a prep problem: five moves that give service hours back, before anyone talks about a new room.

One bar, one list, a spare reach-in: you can do this beside the stick if the stick still has room to work. The moment prep blocks the bartender who is trying to take an order, you have outgrown the corner. That is the point where a layout, power, and water start to matter. Until then, discipline beats square footage.

Equipment checklist

Buy in this order. The top of the table is what a working lab uses every day. The bottom is what a specific menu earns later. Skip a row you cannot name a drink for.

Piece What it is for Skip it if
Gram scale, and a second one Specs, salt, acid, and anything you will have to repeat tomorrow. You will not. Free pouring prep is how a cordial changes flavor by Thursday.
Cold holding that is not the service fridge Juice, dairy, clarifications, and anything with a short life, off the line cooks’ shelf. You have no fresh product. A syrup-only list can live ambient, dated, for a short window.
Stainless table, containers, funnels, a chinois A clean surface and a way to move liquid without a funnel made of a cut bottle. Never. This is the lab.
Heat that can be left and watched Syrups and reductions. Induction is enough for most lists. Every drink is built from bottles. Then you do not have a hot process, and you should not invent one.
Labels, tape, and a marker that survives a cold door Name, date, use-by, initials. The system below. Never.
Vacuum sealer or a tight-lidding backup Batches you want held without a skin, and a way to keep air off a cordial. Your volume is a few bottles a day and the lids actually seal.
Sous vide, centrifuge, rotary evaporator A technique the menu cannot fake with a pot and a filter. You cannot point to the drink. These are specified against throughput on a prep-lab build, not bought to look like a lab.

Smallwares that get forgotten and then borrowed from the kitchen until the kitchen hides them: a ladle with a known volume, sheet pans, quart and liter containers that stack, squeeze bottles for service, and a dedicated trash for peels so the bench stays a bench. Ice is a food. The ice well is not a shelf for backup bottles.

The batching workflow

Pick a window that is not service. Morning, or the dead hour between lunch and dinner. A batch made at 7:30 p.m. because you ran out is an emergency, not a system. One person owns the window. Their name is on the sheet. If everyone owns it, the sour is nobody’s job and it runs out on Saturday.

A batch sheet is one product, one vessel, one recipe in grams, the yield you expect, and the yield you actually got. If those two numbers drift, you have a measuring problem or a recipe problem, and you want to know which before you blame the bartender on the stick. Cool what needs cooling before it is lidded. Hot syrup in a sealed cold box is how you grow something you did not mean to grow.

Service then pulls from finished, labeled stock. The well does not become a second lab at 9 p.m. If a drink still takes four minutes because the prep never happened, the drink is the problem, or the par is. End of night, the same person — or the closer, if you wrote it that way — notes what is low, what hit its date, and what tomorrow’s batch is. That note is the whole production meeting.

Water, acid, and sugar are the three things that move a batch from “tastes right today” to “tastes like a different bar on Friday.” Weigh them. Taste against a retained sample if the product is one you sell a lot of. Write the exception down when you change a ratio, or the next person will “fix” it back.

Labeling

If it is not labeled, it is trash. That sounds severe until you watch a closer sniff a bottle and pour it anyway. The label carries four things: what it is, the day it was made, the day it should be gone, and who made it. Allergens and dairy go on the label in words a new hire can read, not in a code only the head bartender remembers.

Use-by is a decision you make in the daylight, from how the product actually behaves, not a number copied from a blog. Juice is not a syrup. A milk clarification is not a 1:1 simple. When you do not know, the date is shorter, not longer. New stock goes behind old stock. First in, first out only works if the labels face out and the shelf is not a pile.

Transfer a product to a clean container when you top it up, or do not top it up. Pouring today’s batch into the dregs of yesterday’s is how a date on the tape becomes a lie. Squeeze bottles on the bar get the same name and the same date as the parent container. The bar bottle is not a new product with a fresh life.

Storage

Cold and ambient are different shelves, and the decision is about the product, not about how much room you have in the lowboy. Fresh juice, anything dairy, and most clarifications live cold. A plain syrup can live out for a short, written window if the bottle is clean and the room is not a sauna. When in doubt, cold, and a shorter date.

Keep sanitizer, line cleaner, and any other chemical off the same shelf as something a guest will drink. A spray bottle that looks like a syrup bottle will eventually be used as one. Different color, different shelf, labeled as what it is. Do not store prep above clean glass. A drip you do not see becomes a shift you have to reset.

Leave air around containers in the fridge. A lab fridge packed until the door barely shuts is a warm fridge with a light on. Pars exist so you are not storing three weeks of cordial because someone got enthusiastic on a Monday. Product past its date leaves the same day. It does not move to the back “in case.”

Safety

Hot sugar is a burn. Reductions do not get walked away from. A pot of syrup on a busy pass, unattended, is how a lab injures someone who was only trying to reach for a shaker. Heat lives where you can see it, with a clear landing spot for a hot pan that is not the cutting board.

Glass in the ice is a stop. You melt the well. You do not pick the shards out and keep serving. Tell the floor, write it down, and treat it as an incident rather than a mess. Acid, dairy, and nut products get their own containers and a wipe-down that is not the same cloth you just used on the citrus.

Sanitizer is measured. “A glug” is not a concentration, and a sink of it is not a flavor. Follow the dilution on the product you actually buy, and keep the test strips if the product calls for them. Hands get washed at a sink that is not the dump sink, which is also what your health department expects — confirm the local rule before you decide a corner prep station is legal as a food-prep surface. This note is not a health-code filing.

A rotary evaporator, if you ever have one, is not a first purchase and it is not a toy. It needs a person who was trained on it, ventilation, and a reason on the menu. The same is true of anything pressurized or spinning. If the only person who knows the machine quits, the machine is turned off until someone else is trained. Heroics at 1 a.m. are how labs get a reputation they did not want.

Name one person accountable for the lab each day, on the same sheet as the batches. Not a committee. When something smells wrong, that person is allowed to dump it without a meeting.

When the corner is not the lab anymore

This checklist is the operating system for one bar that is willing to be boring about dates and grams. It stops being enough when two or three outlets pull from the same prep, or a hotel needs banquet volume and a lobby bar from the same recipes, or the kitchen has run out of patience with your Cambros. Then you are designing a room: power, water, a flow from dirty to clean, and equipment sized to the Saturday you measured, not to a brochure.

That work is prep-lab design and build. The engagement specs the kit, the drawings a contractor can use, and the production rules. It does not sell you the equipment. The menu that lab exists to serve is the cocktail menu work, and the wider program around it is beverage consulting. Hotel properties that need the lab because the building has more than one bar start at hotel bar consultant, with the lab attached rather than instead of the program.

Questions from the floor

Do I need a separate room to have a cocktail prep lab?

Not at first. A labeled station with cold space, a scale, and a written batch sheet is a lab. A separate room is for volume that no longer fits beside the stick — several outlets, or a hotel drawing from one commissary. That build is a different job from this checklist.

What has to be on a prep label?

The name of what is in the container, the date it was made, the date it should be gone, and the name of the person who made it. If it contains a common allergen, dairy, or a clarification, say so on the label. Unlabeled product gets thrown out. That rule is cheaper than a guess.

Should a new bar buy a rotary evaporator first?

No. Buy the scale, the cold space, the labels, and the containers the menu already needs. A rotary evaporator, centrifuge, or similar tool earns a place when a specific drink cannot be made without it and the labor or the guest response pays for it. Until then it is a catalog item.

When does this checklist stop being enough?

When more than one bar is pulling from the same prep, or the corner of the kitchen is blocking service. At that point you want a designed room: layout, power and water, and production rules sized to the throughput. That engagement is prep-lab design and build.
