How to Price Small-Format Cocktails.

By Andreas Sanidiotis·July 27, 2026·5 min read

Baby pours and cocktail flights are on every 2026 trend list. The demand is real: guests are drinking less and a standard cocktail runs $25 in the big cities. But the format has a trap. Half the liquid does not mean half the cost, and a mini built à la minute takes as long as a full drink. Here is the math that makes small pours work.

Why guests are asking for them

Drinking is at a 90-year low, and guests who still go out are choosier about every pour. I covered the tiering response in the pricing note. Small formats are the other lever. Resy flagged the "tiny 'tini" as a 2026 trend, and Fox News reports venues from Pastis in New York to The Fox Bar in Nashville now carry scaled-down serves. GLP-1 drugs are part of it: operators told the New York Post the drugs cut both appetite and alcohol cravings. The Spirits Business called mini flights one of the five trends shaping 2026, with London bars like Archive & Myth offering every cocktail in half size.

The reference numbers come from Brooklyn. Lele's Roman sells a 2.5 oz baby Negroni at $9 and a flight of three variations at $22. Rosebud in Raleigh is building a $50 cocktail omakase of three small serves. These are not gimmicks. They are pricing structures.

The cost trap

The mistake is pricing a half pour at half price. Liquid cost halves. Nothing else does:

  • Labor is per build, not per milliliter. A mini stirred à la minute is the same five bottles, the same jigger work, the same stir and strain. Bars in the Fox News piece said exactly this: minis take as long to make as full drinks.
  • Glassware, wash cycles, garnish, napkin, ticket time: all fixed per serve.
  • A guest occupying a seat for a $9 drink is occupying the same seat a $16 drink would pay for.

Run the numbers on a Negroni. Full serve: about $2.40 in liquid, $16 on the list, 15% pour cost, $13.60 contribution. Price the baby at $8 (half) and build it à la minute, and you have spent full-drink labor to earn $6.80 of contribution. Do that across a Friday night and the format is costing you money.

The three rules

  • Price a half pour at 55 to 60 percent of the full drink. A $16 Negroni becomes a $9 baby, like Lele's. Pour cost lands around 13%, and the premium over half price covers the fixed costs that did not shrink.
  • Price a trio flight at about 1.4x one full drink. Three half serves is 1.5 drinks of liquid. At $22 against a $16 list price you hold roughly 16% pour cost and take $18.40 of contribution from a seat that would otherwise have produced $13.60. The flight is a check-builder dressed as a tasting.
  • Batch everything small. This is the rule that makes the other two possible. Spirit-forward drinks only, batched with dilution, refrigerated, poured to a line in a chilled glass. Three pours, under a minute, identical every time. If a drink cannot be batched, it does not belong in your small-format program. The prep method is in the prep note.

What to run

Keep it narrow. One flight and two or three babies. The candidates pick themselves:

  • Stirred, spirit-forward, no citrus: Negroni variations, martini riffs, Manhattans, sherry cocktails.
  • Serve up in small coupes or Nick & Nora glasses, around 10 cl. No ice in the glass, ever.
  • Theme the flight so it reads as one decision: three Negronis across three bitters, or three martinis dry to dirty.
  • Put the flight in the menu's sight line and let the babies live under their full-size versions at $9.

Then track it like everything else on the list. A flight with real velocity earns its slot; a baby that nobody orders comes off. The method is in the menu engineering note.

The math on one card

Full Negroni: $2.40 liquid · $16 list · 15% pour cost · $13.60 contribution
Baby (half pour): $1.20 liquid · $9 list (56% of full) · 13% pour cost · $7.80 contribution
Trio flight: $3.60 liquid · $22 list (1.4x full) · 16% pour cost · $18.40 contribution
Build: batched, pre-diluted, refrigerated · poured to a line · under 60 seconds

A small-format program is a pricing and prep exercise, not a menu redesign. If your list needs the structure to hold up first, that is a menu refresh, and fifteen minutes will tell you where to start.
