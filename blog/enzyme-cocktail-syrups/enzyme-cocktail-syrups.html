Enzymes in Cocktail Syrups.

By Andreas Sanidiotis · September 15, 2024 · 6 min read

Enzymes are quietly changing what a bartender can do with fruit. Borrowed from winemaking and juice production, they let you clarify without heat, pull more juice from the same fruit, and develop flavors that filtration and boiling will only flatten. Used well, they are one of the highest-leverage tools in the prep lab.

What an enzyme actually is

An enzyme is a biological catalyst — a protein that speeds up a specific reaction without being consumed by it. Each enzyme is highly selective, acting on one kind of molecule. In the bar, that specificity is the point: you can target the exact compound standing between you and a clearer, brighter, higher-yielding ingredient.

Pectinase: the clarifier

Pectin is the structural gel in fruit that makes juice cloudy and holds liquid inside the pulp. Pectinase breaks pectin down. Add it to fresh juice or macerated fruit, hold it warm (typically around 40–50°C for maximum activity), and two things happen: the juice clarifies as the haze-causing pectin dissolves, and yield jumps because liquid trapped in the pulp is released. For clarified juices and higher extraction from expensive fruit, pectinase is the workhorse.

Amylase and the starch problem

Amylase breaks starch into simpler sugars. It is what lets you work with starchy ingredients — rice, corn, certain root vegetables — without the gluey haze starch leaves behind. It also gently increases perceived sweetness by converting starch to sugar, which opens up cordials and syrups built on ingredients that would otherwise be unusable.

Working with enzymes

  • Temperature. Each enzyme has an optimal range; too cold and it works slowly, too hot and it denatures and dies. A sous-vide bath or a Thermomix at low speed gives the control you want.
  • Time. Enzyme reactions are not instant. Give them the window they need — often 1–4 hours — and judge by clarity and texture, not the clock alone.
  • Dosage. A little goes a long way. Follow the supplier’s ratio, and remember that more enzyme speeds the reaction but does not change the endpoint.
  • Stopping the reaction. A brief heat step denatures the enzyme when you want to lock the result in place.

Why it matters

Heat clarifies and concentrates, but it also cooks — it dulls the fresh, volatile aromatics that make a fruit taste alive. Enzymes let you clarify and extract at low temperature, keeping those aromas intact. The result is a syrup or juice that is clearer, brighter, and more true to the fruit than anything you can boil. For a program built on freshness and precision, that is the whole game.
