Guests Read Your Menu Like a Book. Design for It.

By Andreas Sanidiotis·August 10, 2026·5 min read

Eye-tracking research puts the average time a guest spends with a menu at about 109 seconds. In that time they read it the way they read a book: start to finish, section by section, in order. There is no magic corner where eyes land and orders happen. A lot of menu advice still sells one. What actually moves the order is simpler and free: where a drink sits in its section, what sits above it, and how the price is written.

The sweet spot is a myth

For decades the industry taught that eyes jump to a sweet spot above the center of the right page, so that is where the money items should go. In 2012 Sybil Yang, then at San Francisco State after starting the work at Cornell, tracked diners' eyes with an infrared camera while they read a menu and ordered. Result: gazes moved through the menu sequentially, like a book, and did not linger on any hot zone. No sweet spot existed. Yang's own conclusion was that the industry had been repeating old research that never held up.

She did find the opposite: a sour spot, a dead zone where gazes spent the least time. The practical lesson is not where the magic is. It is that reading order is the whole game. If guests read top to bottom, the first and last items in each section get the most attention, and the middle of a long list is where drinks go to be skipped.

What actually moves the order

  • Slot position. First and last place in each section carry the attention. Put your two highest contribution-margin drinks there. Margin in dollars, not pour cost percent: the math is in Your POS Knows Which Cocktails to Cut.
  • One anchor. Cornell research on price anchoring found that when a higher-priced item leads, spending on what follows rises. Your $22 statement drink is not there to sell volume. It is there to make the $17 core list feel easy. The tier structure is in Guests Are Drinking Less. Price for It.
  • No currency symbols. A Cornell CHR field study in an upscale casual restaurant found guests ordering from a menu with plain numerals spent $5.55 more per cover, about 8%, than guests seeing dollar signs. Write 17, not $17.00. And nest the price at the end of the description in the same type size. A right-aligned price column invites reading the menu by price, top to bottom.
  • One box. A box or white space around an item pulls the eye. Two boxes cancel each other. One per page, on the drink with the best margin, and the drink has to deserve the look.
  • Seven to nine per section. Guests seriously consider a handful of items, not a page of them. Hold each section to seven to nine. Past that you are paying for indecision.

The 109-second budget

Read time is roughly fixed. Every item you add divides it. A 24-cocktail list gives each drink about four seconds of consideration; a nine-drink list gives each one twelve. The dead drinks you keep for sentiment are charging rent in seconds, paid by the drinks that earn.

Sequential reading also means section order is a decision. Whatever guests read first frames everything after it. Open the menu with the list you make the most on, usually the house cocktails, with the anchor at the top. Beer and wine can follow. If the first thing guests read is the cheapest section, you priced the whole read downward.

The one-page audit

Run this on your current menu

1. Count each section. Cut to seven to nine items.
2. Move the two highest-margin drinks to the first and last slots.
3. Lead the menu with the section that earns the most.
4. Strip currency symbols. Nest prices in the description.
5. Keep one box, on the drink with the best margin.
6. Rerun your sales mix in 30 days and compare.

None of this touches a recipe or a price. It costs one reprint. Against a sales-mix engineering benchmark of 8 to 14% margin improvement per quarter, the reprint is the cheapest work you will do on the program this year.

A menu refresh, the list, the pricing, and the physical page, is its own engagement: Menu & Program Refresh. If your list is 24 drinks with dollar signs down the right edge, fifteen minutes will tell you what to fix first.
